WEEK 7 · FREE FALLING BODIES AND PROJECTILE MOTION

Can We Predict the Catch?

Core ideas

  • Free fall means gravity is the only significant force. Near Earth, g = 9.80 m/s². In the ideal model, mass does not set the fall acceleration.
  • With upward positive, ay = −g. At the top of a toss, vy = 0, but the acceleration is still −g.
  • Projectile motion has two coordinated components: constant horizontal velocity and constant vertical acceleration. They share the same time.
  • A horizontal launch traces a parabola. An angled launch starts with components v₀cosθ and v₀sinθ.
  • Launch angle changes the trajectory. A longer flight need not travel farther. Two angles can give the same ideal range.
  • Range formulas depend on endpoint heights (launch and landing at the same level).
  • A perception claim about catching needs perceptual evidence, not only a physics model.

Key formulas

Velocityvy = v0y − gt
Positiony = y₀ + v0yt − ½gt²
Componentsv0x = v₀ cosθ, v0y = v₀ sinθ
Same-height flightT = 2v₀ sinθ / g, R = v₀² sin2θ / g
Worked anchorDropped from rest through 19.6 m: t = 2.00 s, v = −19.6 m/s. Launched at 10.0 m/s, 30.0°: T ≈ 1.02 s and R ≈ 8.84 m (same-height landing).
Common traps
  • Mixing signs when upward is positive.
  • Using the same-height range formula when the endpoints differ.
  • Treating the peak as zero acceleration.
Psychology linkCatching requires prediction and correction by the nervous system. The ideal model describes the ball, not the perceiver.
WEEK 8 · IMPULSE, MOMENTUM, AND COLLISIONS

What Changes in a Collision?

Core ideas

  • Momentum is mass times velocity and is a vector, so its sign matters.
  • Impulse is the signed change in momentum. The area under a force–time graph equals impulse.
  • For the same change in momentum, a longer stopping time means a smaller average force.
  • Momentum is conserved for a defined system when external impulse is negligible. Always check this first.
  • Collision types: elastic (kinetic energy conserved) and inelastic (kinetic energy changes). In a sticking collision the objects share one final velocity.
  • In recoil, objects starting at rest keep total momentum at zero.
  • TBI: rapid head motion can load tissue (coup and contrecoup). A collision model has limits.

Key formulas

Momentump = mv
ImpulseJ = Favg Δt = Δp
Conservationm₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁u₁ + m₂u₂
Sticking collisionv = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂) / (m₁ + m₂)
Worked anchor1.00 kg at +3.00 m/s sticks to a stationary 2.00 kg: v = 1.00 m/s. Kinetic energy falls from 4.50 J to 1.50 J while momentum is conserved. Stopping a 0.500 kg cart from +4.00 m/s in 0.200 s: Δp = −2.00 N·s, Favg = −10.0 N.
Common traps
  • Forgetting to choose a positive direction first.
  • Saying momentum is lost in an inelastic collision (kinetic energy changes; system momentum does not).
  • Applying conservation without checking external impulse.
Psychology linkCollision mechanics can explain how injury forces arise, but not by themselves the psychological effects that follow.
WEEK 9 · CIRCULAR MOTION AND TORQUE

Why Does Turning Require Force?

Core ideas

  • Turning changes the direction of velocity, so a steady speed can still include acceleration.
  • Period T is the time for one turn; frequency f = 1/T. Speed along the circle is circumference over period.
  • Angular speed ω links angle and time (rad/s). Points farther from the axis move faster at the same ω.
  • Centripetal acceleration points inward. At fixed radius, doubling the speed quadruples it.
  • An inward net force causes it. “Centripetal” names the force’s role; the real source (tension, friction, normal force, gravity) depends on the system.
  • A change in speed adds tangential acceleration.
  • The outward feeling in a turning car comes from an accelerating frame. Perceived motion combines several signals.

Key formulas

Circular speedv = 2πr / T
Angular speedω = 2π / T = 2πf, v = ωr
Inward accelerationac = v² / r = ω²r
Inward forceFc = m v² / r
Worked anchorA point moving at 2.00 m/s on a circle of radius 0.500 m has ac = 4.00 / 0.500 = 8.00 m/s², directed toward the centre. Ten turns in a mean 20.0 s gives T = 2.00 s.
Common traps
  • Treating centripetal force as a separate new force on a free-body diagram.
  • Using centimetres for radius instead of metres.
  • Forgetting that velocity changes even at constant speed.
Psychology linkThe feeling of being pushed outward in a turn is a physical tendency to move straight, interpreted by several sensory signals.
WEEK 10 · ROTATIONAL DYNAMICS

What Changes a Spin?

Core ideas

  • Torque is a force’s turning effect about a chosen axis. The axis is part of the definition.
  • Torque depends on force, location, and angle. A radial force has zero torque about the centre.
  • Choose a sign convention (for example counterclockwise positive) for net torque.
  • Net torque changes angular velocity. Rotational inertia I depends on mass distribution: moving mass outward increases I.
  • A rigid disk has one angular speed, but the path speed still depends on radius.
  • Rotational kinetic energy varies with angular speed squared.
  • Angular momentum is conserved when net external torque is zero, so smaller I means larger ω (a tuck).

Key formulas

Torqueτ = rF sinθ = r⊥F
Net torqueΣτ = Iα
Rotational inertiaI = Σ m r² (point masses)
Rotational energyKErot = ½ I ω²
Angular momentumL = Iω
Worked anchorA perpendicular 10.0 N push at 0.80 m gives τ = 8.00 N·m. I = 0.500 kg·m² at ω = 4.00 rad/s gives KErot = 4.00 J. If L is conserved and I is halved, ω doubles.
Common traps
  • Not stating the rotation axis and sign convention.
  • Using degrees where radians are required.
  • Using L conservation when an external torque acts.
Psychology linkThe semicircular canals sense head rotation, but perceived rotation can fade during sustained spin even though the physical rotation continues.
WEEK 11 · WORK, ENERGY, AND POTENTIAL ENERGY

Where Does the Energy Go?

Core ideas

  • Work depends on the force component along the displacement. It can be positive, negative, or zero.
  • Kinetic energy depends on speed squared, so doubling speed quadruples it.
  • Work–energy theorem: net work equals the change in kinetic energy.
  • Gravitational potential energy needs a height datum. Spring energy grows with displacement squared.
  • For gravity-and-spring models, mechanical energy is KE plus PE. State the system and the reference height.
  • Energy converts between forms; it does not disappear. A pendulum trades height and speed.
  • Holding a bag does no mechanical work on it without displacement. Mental effort needs psychological evidence, not just a physics term.

Key formulas

Work (constant force)W = F d cosθ
Kinetic energyKE = ½ m v²
Work–energyWnet = ΔKE
Gravitational PEPEg = m g h
Spring PEPEs = ½ k x²
Worked anchorRaising a 2.00 kg object 0.500 m at constant speed: ΔPE = 2.00 × 9.80 × 0.500 = 9.80 J. A 2.00 kg cart at 1.00 m/s has KE = 1.00 J. A spring with k = 100 N/m compressed 0.100 m stores 0.500 J.
Common traps
  • Forgetting the cosθ factor (force at an angle).
  • Changing the height datum partway through a problem.
  • Equating physical work with mental effort.
Psychology linkThe brain has energy needs even at rest. Whether mental fatigue has a biological basis must be shown by research evidence.
