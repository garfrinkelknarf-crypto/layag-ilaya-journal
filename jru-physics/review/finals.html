WEEK 13 · DENSITY, PRESSURE, AND FLUID MECHANICS

Why Does Pressure Change?

Core ideas

  • Density compares mass with volume.
  • Pressure compares normal force with area. Fluid pressure acts perpendicular to a surface.
  • In a resting liquid of constant density, pressure increases with depth. It depends on depth and fluid density, not container shape. Connected liquid columns reach the same level.
  • Absolute pressure includes atmospheric pressure; gauge pressure is relative to it. A manometer compares pressures from a height difference.
  • Pascal’s principle: a pressure change in an enclosed fluid is transmitted. A hydraulic system trades force for distance.
  • Continuity for steady incompressible flow: a narrower tube means faster flow.
  • Flow is driven by a pressure difference through resistance. Acute stress can temporarily raise blood pressure.

Key formulas

Densityρ = m / V
Pressurep = F / A (1 Pa = 1 N/m²)
Pressure vs depthp = p₀ + ρgh
Pascal / hydraulicF₂ / A₂ = F₁ / A₁
ContinuityA₁v₁ = A₂v₂
Worked anchor0.160 kg of liquid in 200 cm³ (2.00 × 10⁻⁴ m³): ρ = 800 kg/m³. Water 2.00 m deep: Δp = 1000 × 9.80 × 2.00 = 1.96 × 10⁴ Pa.
Common traps
  • Forgetting that 1 cm³ = 10⁻⁶ m³.
  • Mixing up absolute and gauge pressure.
  • Assuming a wider container gives more pressure at the same depth.
Psychology linkA flow model of blood pressure can describe physical changes under stress, but it needs a defined physical model to be useful.
WEEK 14 · BUOYANT FORCES AND ARCHIMEDES’ PRINCIPLE

How Can Fluids Support the Brain?

Core ideas

  • Even a sinking object receives an upward buoyant force. It comes from the pressure difference between lower and upper surfaces.
  • Archimedes’ principle: the buoyant force equals the weight of the displaced fluid.
  • Compare buoyant force with weight to predict motion. Average density helps predict floating.
  • A floating object at rest has buoyant force equal to its weight, which fixes the submerged fraction.
  • A loaded ship displaces more water; buoyant force grows with displaced volume.
  • Apparent weight is the true weight minus the buoyant force. It can reveal volume.
  • Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) supports the brain by reducing the load on surrounding tissue. The model has a limited biological scope.

Key formulas

Buoyant forceFB = ρfluid · g · Vdisplaced
Floating at restFB = W
Submerged fractionVsub / V = ρobject / ρfluid
Apparent weightWapp = W − FB
Worked anchorAn object displacing 200 cm³ of water feels FB = 1000 × 9.80 × 2.00 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.96 N. A uniform block of 600 kg/m³ in water floats with 60% of its volume submerged.
Common traps
  • Using total volume when only part of the object is submerged.
  • Mixing object density with fluid density.
  • Thinking a sinking object has no buoyant force.
Psychology linkCSF illustrates a physical principle in biology: the brain is partly supported by fluid, which lowers its effective weight.
WEEK 15 · TEMPERATURE AND THERMODYNAMICS

What Does a Thermometer Tell Us?

Core ideas

  • Temperature, heat, and internal energy are different quantities. Heat Q is energy transferred because of a temperature difference.
  • Celsius and Kelvin have equal-sized intervals, so a temperature change is the same in both. Use Kelvin where required.
  • Thermometers use temperature-dependent properties. The zeroth law supports comparing temperatures through thermal equilibrium.
  • Specific heat links energy and temperature change. Equal energy can warm different materials differently.
  • Heat capacity includes the amount of material.
  • Thermal expansion needs room to move. A bimetallic strip bends because two materials expand differently.
  • Body temperature reflects an energy balance. A sensation of hot or cold does not by itself measure thermal energy.

Key formulas

KelvinT(K) = T(°C) + 273.15
Heat (no phase change)Q = m c ΔT
Heat capacityC = m c
Linear expansionΔL = α L₀ ΔT
Worked anchorWarming 0.200 kg of water from 20.0 °C to 25.0 °C with c = 4186 J/(kg·K): Q = 0.200 × 4186 × 5.00 ≈ 4.19 × 10³ J. A 1.00 m rod with α = 12.0 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ warmed by 50.0 K lengthens by 6.00 × 10⁻⁴ m.
Common traps
  • Treating temperature and heat as the same thing.
  • Adding 273.15 to a temperature difference.
  • Using Q = mcΔT during a phase change.
Psychology linkTouch reports heat exchange with the skin, not the object’s temperature directly.
WEEK 16 · HEAT AND HEAT EXCHANGE

How Does the Body Exchange Heat?

Core ideas

  • Energy flows spontaneously from hotter to colder until equilibrium.
  • In an insulated mixing model, heat gained by one sample equals heat lost by the other.
  • First law: for a closed system, the change in internal energy equals heat added minus work done by the system.
  • During a phase change, energy is absorbed or released at steady temperature. Latent heat relates energy to the mass changed.
  • Heat crosses boundaries by conduction, convection, and radiation, often together.
  • Sweating cools through evaporation. A refrigerator moves heat using external work.
  • Thermoregulation: net storage equals gains minus losses.

Key formulas

MixingΣ m c ΔT = 0
First lawΔU = Q − W
Phase changeQ = m L
RefrigeratorQhot = Qcold + W
Worked anchorMixing 0.100 kg of water at 60.0 °C with 0.200 kg at 30.0 °C: Tf = (0.100×60.0 + 0.200×30.0) / 0.300 = 40.0 °C. A system that absorbs 500 J of heat and does 200 J of work has ΔU = +300 J.
Common traps
  • Mixing up sign conventions for Q and W (Q positive into the system, W positive when the system does work).
  • Expecting temperature to change during a phase change.
  • Forgetting the system boundary.
Psychology linkBody heat regulation is an energy balance with several mechanisms. A sensation alone does not quantify thermal energy.
WEEK 17 · NUCLEAR PHYSICS AND BEYOND

How Can Physics Reveal the Brain?

Core ideas

  • The nucleus contains protons and neutrons. Atomic number Z identifies the element; mass number A = Z + N.
  • Isotopes share Z but differ in neutron number. Radiation forms: alpha, beta, gamma.
  • Half-life: each half-life halves what remains of the parent nuclei. Fission splits a heavy nucleus; reactions can release energy.
  • PET detects photons from positron annihilation after a radiotracer decays. MRI uses nuclear magnetic resonance of nuclear spin. They detect different physical signals.
  • Special relativity: the laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames, and the speed of light in vacuum is the same for all observers.
  • The Lorentz factor depends on relative speed. Proper time is measured by one clock at one location; a moving clock’s interval is stretched.
  • Length contraction applies along the direction of motion. Physical time dilation differs from felt duration, which needs psychological evidence.

Key formulas

NuclideA = Z + N
Half-life decayN = N₀ (½)^(t / T½)
Lorentz factorγ = 1 / √(1 − v²/c²)
Time dilationΔt = γ Δt₀
Length contractionL = L₀ / γ
Worked anchorAt v = 0.600c, γ = 1.25. A clock interval of 8.00 μs becomes 10.0 μs; a 10.0 m rod appears 8.00 m long. With T½ = 10.0 min, one-eighth remains after 30.0 min.
Common traps
  • Not stating the observer’s frame in relativity.
  • Applying contraction to lengths perpendicular to the motion.
  • Treating felt duration as a clock measurement.
Psychology linkA clock measurement and a person’s felt duration are different kinds of evidence.
