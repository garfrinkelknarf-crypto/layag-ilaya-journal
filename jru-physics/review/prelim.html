WEEK 1 · SCIENTIFIC METHOD AND MEASUREMENT

Can We Trust What We Measure?

Core ideas

  • Physics connects to psychology through measurable quantities: time, intensity, force, distance.
  • Scientific inquiry is a revisable process: question, hypothesis, fair test, data, conclusion. A fair comparison changes one thing at a time.
  • Psychophysics relates a physical change to the change a person perceives. Weber’s law: the just-noticeable difference grows in proportion to the starting intensity.
  • The seven SI base units: metre (m), kilogram (kg), second (s), ampere (A), kelvin (K), mole (mol), candela (cd).
  • Accuracy is closeness to a reference value. Precision is closeness of repeated measurements to each other (spread).
  • A useful measurement states the quantity, the unit, and the method.

Key formulas

Weber’s lawΔI / I = k (JND: ΔI = k·I)
Meanx̄ = Σx / n
Rangerange = max − min
Percent error|measured − reference| / reference × 100%
Worked anchorOffset and spread are different things. Timing data can have a mean close to the reference (accurate) and still have a wide range (not precise), or the reverse. A mean alone can hide this.
Common traps
  • Accurate and precise are not synonyms.
  • A small percent error does not guarantee small spread.
  • A number without a unit or method is not a complete measurement.
Psychology linkWeber’s law explains why the same 1 kg increase is noticed on a light object but not on a heavy one: perception tracks proportional change.
WEEK 2 · UNITS, SIGNIFICANT FIGURES, AND SAFETY

How Much Detail Can Data Support?

Core ideas

  • Dimensions (length, time, mass) describe the kind of quantity; units give the scale.
  • Metric prefixes: kilo 10³, centi 10⁻², milli 10⁻³, micro 10⁻⁶.
  • A conversion factor equals 1 (for example 1 s = 1000 ms). Choose the version that makes the unwanted unit cancel. Area and volume need the factor raised to the same power.
  • Scientific notation separates value and scale: a × 10ⁿ with 1 ≤ a < 10.
  • Significant figures: non-zero digits count, zeros between digits count, leading zeros do not, trailing zeros after a decimal point do.
  • Multiply or divide: keep the fewest significant figures. Add or subtract: keep the fewest decimal places. Round only at the end.
  • Choose the tool for the quantity, record the method, and follow safe and ethical measurement practice.

Key formulas

Conversionnew value = old value × (new unit / old unit)
Scientific notationN = a × 10ⁿ, 1 ≤ a < 10
Powered factors1 m² = 10⁴ cm², 1 m³ = 10⁶ cm³
Worked anchor245 ms = 0.245 s (multiply by 1 s / 1000 ms). 4.50 km/h = 4.50 × 1000 m / 3600 s = 1.25 m/s.
Common traps
  • Rounding in the middle of a calculation.
  • Using a length factor for an area or volume without squaring or cubing it.
  • Counting leading zeros as significant.
Psychology linkA reaction-time display may show many digits. The method sets how many the data can honestly support.
WEEK 3 · VECTORS

Where Did the Gaze Move?

Core ideas

  • A scalar has magnitude only. A vector has magnitude and direction.
  • Displacement compares the endpoints. A return to the start gives zero displacement even after a long path.
  • On one axis, direction is carried by the sign. State your convention.
  • Graphical addition is tail to head; the resultant joins the start of the first vector to the end of the last.
  • Components describe the same vector as two perpendicular parts. Components add independently along each axis.
  • Subtraction adds the opposite vector: A − B = A + (−B).
  • Direction needs a quadrant check. A frame of reference gives directions their meaning.

Key formulas

ComponentsAx = A cosθ, Ay = A sinθ (θ from +x)
MagnitudeA = √(Ax² + Ay²)
Directionθ = tan⁻¹(Ay / Ax) + quadrant check
ResultantRx = Ax + Bx, Ry = Ay + By
Worked anchor3.0 cm east then 4.0 cm north gives a resultant of 5.0 cm at 53° north of east (tan⁻¹(4.0/3.0) ≈ 53°).
Common traps
  • Letting the calculator choose the wrong quadrant.
  • Confusing path length with net displacement.
  • Mixing degrees and radians.
Psychology linkA gaze path can be long while its net displacement is small. The two numbers answer different questions.
WEEK 4 · KINEMATICS IN ONE AND TWO DIMENSIONS

What Does Motion Tell Us?

Core ideas

  • Motion is always described relative to a reference frame.
  • Displacement Δx = xf − xi. Average velocity is displacement per elapsed time. Average speed is distance per time, so the two can differ.
  • Instantaneous velocity describes one moment: the slope of the position–time graph at that moment.
  • Acceleration is the change in velocity per time. Same signs for v and a: speeding up. Opposite signs: slowing down.
  • Slope of a velocity–time graph is acceleration. Area under it is displacement.
  • A change in direction is an acceleration, even at constant speed.
  • The vestibular system senses acceleration, not constant velocity.

Key formulas

DisplacementΔx = xf − xi
Average velocityv̄ = Δx / Δt
Average accelerationā = Δv / Δt
2D average velocityv̄ = (Δx/Δt, Δy/Δt)
Worked anchorA cart slows from +6.0 m/s to rest in 3.0 s: a = (0 − 6.0) / 3.0 = −2.0 m/s².
Common traps
  • Negative acceleration does not always mean slowing down.
  • A position–time graph shows position over time, not the shape of the path.
  • Using speed when the question asks for velocity.
Psychology linkPhysical motion and perceived motion are not the same: the vestibular system responds to changes in motion.
WEEK 5 · KINEMATICS (CONT’D) AND NEWTON’S LAWS

What Changes Our Motion?

Core ideas

  • Motion diagrams: equal gaps in equal time mean constant speed; changing gaps mean acceleration.
  • The constant-acceleration equations apply only when a is constant. In two dimensions, x and y share one clock.
  • First law: zero net force means zero acceleration (constant velocity).
  • A free-body diagram shows the forces acting on one chosen system. Typical forces: weight, normal, friction, tension, applied.
  • Second law: net force sets acceleration; for the same net force, more mass means less acceleration.
  • Third law: partner forces are equal and opposite and act on different objects.
  • Biomechanics: in walking, the ground and the foot exert forces on each other.

Key formulas

Velocityv = v₀ + at
Positionx = x₀ + v₀t + ½at²
No time givenv² = v₀² + 2a(x − x₀)
Second lawΣF = ma
Worked anchorv₀ = +2.0 m/s, a = +2.0 m/s², t = 3.0 s: v = 8.0 m/s and Δx = 15 m. A net force of 6.0 N on 2.0 kg gives 3.0 m/s².
Common traps
  • Using constant-acceleration equations when a changes.
  • Treating third-law partners as cancelling inside one free-body diagram.
  • Drawing forces that this object exerts on others.
Psychology linkExplaining a movement needs forces and evidence, not effort alone.
